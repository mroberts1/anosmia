## What it is

Every viewer of a perfume commercial is in the position of someone with anosmia — structurally deprived of the sense being advertised. No audio-visual medium can transmit smell. The product's defining quality is absent from every ad for it by definition. Halszka Leleń names this "communicative anosmia" and takes it as the starting condition that explains why perfume ads look the way they do.

The problem is compounded by timing: campaigns often launch before the product reaches stores, so viewers cannot even run out and smell it immediately after seeing the ad.

## Why it matters

Communicative anosmia is explanatory, not merely descriptive. It tells you *why* perfume advertising converged on sophisticated narrative immersion strategies rather than product demonstration or testimonial. The ads can't show the product working — they have to build a world so absorbing that the viewer incorporates the perfume as part of their own identity story. This is a structural constraint, not an aesthetic choice.

## Key points

- Every perfume ad viewer is structurally anosmic with respect to the product being advertised
- Ads compensate via narrative perspectivization: placing viewers inside stories that engage them cognitively, emotionally, and sensorially
- Directors do not attempt to describe the scent directly; they substitute immersive fictional worlds
- The gap between commercial and purchase creates a second lag — the viewer may smell the product weeks after seeing the ad
- Synesthesia is the core tool: sight, sound, touch, and proprioception are recruited to suggest an absent smell
- Body-in-movement becomes a visual surrogate for fragrance diffusion: Gisele surfing through foam, Rihanna emerging from feathers, Qualley convulsing through a ballroom
- Tsutsumi notes NCT DOJAEJUNG's direct response to the problem: blotter papers with the members' personal scents shipped inside physical album copies — tactile/olfactory engagement the video cannot provide^[#source/secondary]

## Evidence across sources

Leleń establishes the concept as the interpretive frame for all perfume commercial analysis. The specific examples — palindrome structure (Reb'l Fleur), bottle-body identification through circular montage (Miss Dior), surfing body as water-in-diffusion (Chanel 2014) — all follow the same logic: replace the absent smell with a sensory-narrative equivalent.

Tsutsumi extends this to the K-Pop case, noting that the "Perfume" music video deploys the same compensating strategies (nature imagery, floral surroundings, fragrance-linked lyrics) while adding the blotter paper as a literal correction to communicative anosmia.
