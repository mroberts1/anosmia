# Technostalgia

**Summary**: The aesthetics of archaic analog sound — heard as nostalgic, historically charged, or hauntological — that intensified after the late 2000s as digital listening made analog noise more perceptible, novel, and meaningful.

**Sources**: `What was Lo-Fi .pdf`

**Last updated**: 2026-04-25

---

## Overview

"Technostalgia" describes the affective and aesthetic significance that accrues to older sound reproduction technologies and their characteristic noises — tape hiss, vinyl crackle, limited frequency response — when heard from a digital-era listening position. The term is used by Craig Eley and others; it connects the analog sonic palette with nostalgia for a (real or imagined) past. (source: `What was Lo-Fi .pdf`)

Harper notes that digital technologies made analog noise *more* perceptible and meaningful than it had been in earlier decades: "hearing analogue noise itself [became] more novel, more perceptible, more meaningful, and more marked as being of imperfect quality than it had been in earlier decades." (source: `What was Lo-Fi .pdf`)

This was not inevitable. A 1961 review of Robert Johnson's recordings heard them as "amazingly clear, brilliant, and lifelike for their time." By the 2010s, Mark Fisher and Owen Hatherley heard "layers upon layers of fizz, crackle, hiss, white noise." Same recordings; different historicized positions. (source: `What was Lo-Fi .pdf`)

## Hauntology

Mark Fisher popularized "hauntology" for music marked by archaic sounds and a sense of lost futures — music that sounds like it is haunted by the past. Fisher associated it with acts including Burial, the Caretaker, Little Axe, and the Ghost Box record label. The term draws on Derrida and applies nostalgia theory to electronic and avant-garde music in the late 2000s. (source: `What was Lo-Fi .pdf`)

The "dramatic irony" of technostalgia: "the noise of the medium... was never meant to be noticed" (Thompson). William Basinski's *Disintegration Loops* — recording the literal crumbling of analog tape onto digital — is the canonical example. (source: `What was Lo-Fi .pdf`)

## Adjacent genre terms

Several genre labels emerged from the late 2000s combining archaic analog sound with archaic musical idioms (often suggesting 1980s or 1990s media):

- **Hypnagogic pop** — coined by David Keenan in *The Wire* (2009); hazy, degraded recordings of 80s pop idioms
- **Chillwave** — Washed Out, Memory Cassette; soft analog warmth with pop melodies
- **Glo-fi** — e.g., Small Black
- **Vaporwave** — slowed, pitched-down samples of 1980s/90s music; elevator music aesthetics; lo-fi and nostalgic aspects explored by Laura Glitsos

(source: `What was Lo-Fi .pdf`)

## Connection to lo-fi

Technostalgia and [[lo-fi]] overlap significantly after the late 2000s: acts that adopted archaic analog sounds also adopted archaic musical idioms, and lo-fi's aesthetics of recording imperfection provided a receptive context. But technostalgia extends well beyond the indie rock lo-fi canon — into electronic music, hip hop, and avant-garde music that the 1990s category excluded.

## Analog formats today

Harper notes that analog sound reproduction formats (vinyl, tape) remain popular for sound quality, physicality, and even noise. But appreciation of analog sound does not necessarily mean appreciating "poor sound quality" — it is not straightforwardly lo-fi. The relationship between analog preference and lo-fi aesthetics is open. (source: `What was Lo-Fi .pdf`)
