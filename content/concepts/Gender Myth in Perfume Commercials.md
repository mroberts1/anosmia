## What it is

Perfume commercials construct specific gender ideals — heteronormative, bourgeois, white masculinity and femininity — and present them as natural. This is the "false obvious": constructed meaning that has ceased to appear constructed. The ads function ideologically not by argument but by repetition and saturation; the gender norms are encoded so densely across so many commercials that they come to seem like descriptions of reality rather than prescriptions.

The theoretical lineage: Barthes (myth naturalized as fact), Hall (representation as meaning production), Butler (gender as repeated stylization of the body). Perfume ads provide the templates for that stylization.

## Why it matters

Because perfume's appeal is sold as sexual and personal, perfume ads are more heavily gender-coded than most advertising categories. They constitute a concentrated site where gender ideology is not merely reproduced but actively performed at high aesthetic quality and production value — making the mythology harder to refuse.

## Key points

- Barthes: myth transforms history into nature; the gender norms in perfume ads achieve "false obvious" status — appearing self-evident rather than chosen
- Hall: representation works through two systems — conceptual maps and the signs/language that encode them; perfume ads link erotic symbols to specific gender concepts
- Butler: "Gender is the repeated stylization of the body, a set of repeated acts within a highly rigid regulatory frame" — perfume ads supply both the acts and the frame^[#source/peer-reviewed]
- Male coding: toned skin, beards, abdominal muscles, suits; physical dominance, sexual conquest, outdoor power
- Female coding: silk dresses, stilettos, bare collarbones and breasts; object of Mulvey's erotic gaze; "sensual figures that need to be sexually conquered"
- Water imagery as gendered: green lake = heterosexual desire; raging sea = male power and penetration; sweat and rain = aroused wet body
- Content analysis (45 ads, Deng & Shi): 73% showed direct sexual behavior or explicit sexual implication; 64% physical nudity; 33 contained intimate heterosexual interaction^[#source/peer-reviewed]
- Miss Dior (Portman) commercial: appears to celebrate female independence (leaving wedding, reclaiming "miss"); symptomatic reading reveals the "independent" femininity is still structured to attract male desire
- J'adore (Theron): dual mythological layers — naked erotic body (first level) → "Golden Goddess of independence" (second level); female viewers identify with an ideal that serves heteronormative looking
- Butler: aspiring to be or own the "she" of the goddess commercial places viewers inside heteronormative binary — becoming practitioners of the norms
- NCT DOJAEJUNG "Perfume" MV subverts male cologne conventions via "flower boy" aesthetic: jewelry, silk, florals, emotional vulnerability; none of the dominance-and-conquest coding^[#source/secondary]

## Evidence across sources

Deng & Shi provide the primary framework and the content-analysis data. Tsutsumi provides the K-Pop counter-case that shows the male gender convention is not structurally necessary — NCT "Perfume" abandons the muscular conquest model entirely while remaining a fragrance-adjacent product. The contrast sharpens what the Western convention is actively constructing.

One tension worth tracking: Leleń's analysis of Kenzo World (Spike Jonze, 2016) treats it as a genuine subversion of the erotic-body norm — the convulsive non-feminine dance, the "inability to conform as power" — without engaging the question of whether it escapes ideological capture. Deng & Shi would likely read Kenzo World's "rebellious goddess" as a more sophisticated version of the same mythology. This is a difference in interpretive frame rather than a factual conflict, and neither source directly addresses the other's claim. Note the tension rather than resolving it.
