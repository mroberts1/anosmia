## What it is

Perfume commercials characteristically layer multiple narrative traditions simultaneously — fairy tales, classical myths, literary texts, cinematic genres, celebrity intertexts — so that any given ad contains several overlapping "texts" the viewer reads at once. Leleń calls this "palimpsestic" after the medieval manuscript practice of writing over existing text while leaving earlier inscriptions partially legible.

The mechanism: viewers recognize the embedded story patterns and automatically fill in gaps, activating their own cultural associations. This recognition-and-inference process is what creates intersubjective immersion — viewers are inside the story, not watching it. Coleridge's "willing suspension of disbelief" is induced precisely because the patterns are familiar.

## Why it matters

Palimpsestic narrative is the primary technique by which communicative anosmia is addressed. Because the smell cannot be transmitted, the ad builds a cognitive-emotional world dense enough that the viewer projects themselves into it — and into the experience of wearing the perfume. The richer the palimpsest, the more pathways the viewer has into the story.

## Key points

- The chief strategy is not originality but "formulaic but attractive" new combinations of known narrative patterns
- Rihanna Reb'l Fleur (2011): Alice Through the Looking-Glass + Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde + phoenix myth; palindromic structure playable both ways; slogans flip on each mirror-crossing ("Bad Feels so Good" / "Good Feels so Bad"); symbolist good-evil duality traceable to Baudelaire
- Chanel No 5 (2004, Luhrmann/Kidman, $33M): intertextual with Moulin Rouge! and Eyes Wide Shut; celebrity associations are themselves a palimpsest of Kidman's romantic roles; "vampire effect" risk acknowledged
- Chanel No 5 (2014, Bündchen): siren emergence from water + Great Gatsby bridge + romance narrative; Gisele's body spiraling through wave and air is the visual surrogate for fragrance diffusion
- Kenzo World (2016, Spike Jonze): Eve rebellion + medieval chivalric romance (Sir Gawain and the Green Knight, emerald-green color coding) + mirror/gaze motifs + modern ballroom; "inability to conform is her power"
- Miss Dior (2019, Portman): Gertrude Stein's "Sacred Emily" ("Rose is a rose is a rose") recited as poem; circular montage identifies perfume bottle visually with Portman's body; product and person become one
- YSL Black Opium (2018, Zoë Kravitz): Tokyo nightlife + "The Hills" (The Weeknd) + temple-dance choreography; urban corruption as inherited Baudelairean symbolism
- Synesthesia is the poetic device underlying the strategy: Baudelaire's "Correspondences" — "There are perfumes as cool as the flesh of children / Sweet as oboes, green as meadows" — establishes multi-sensory equivalences that perfume ads inherit
- Tsutsumi: NCT DOJAEJUNG's "Perfume" MV operates within the palimpsest tradition: nature aspirational visual layer + explicit Marcel Proust literary reference (the members brand themselves "Proust House"); scent-and-memory lyrics; floral imagery from female-marketed perfume conventions^[#source/secondary]
- Celebrity in perfume ads adds a personal palimpsest layer: intertextual associations from the star's other roles; K-Pop extends this via parasocial attachment — fans have pre-existing emotional investment that intensifies the associations

## Evidence across sources

Leleń provides the concept and the close-reading examples with scholarly detail. Tsutsumi applies the logic to a K-Pop case, treating the "Perfume" MV's Proust reference and nature-aspiration visuals as deliberate palimpsest choices. The two sources are complementary rather than in conflict.

## Open questions

- Are there perfume campaigns that succeed through narrative *simplicity* rather than palimpsest — and what accounts for them?
- Is the palimpsest strategy unique to fragrance, or is it the default mode of high-production lifestyle advertising generally?
- How do audiences who don't share the Western literary canon (Baudelaire, Carroll, Fitzgerald) receive ads built on those intertexts?
