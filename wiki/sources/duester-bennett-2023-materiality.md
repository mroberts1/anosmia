# How Does Materiality 'Bite Back'? — Duester & Bennett (2023)

**Summary**: Uses Magaudda's "circuit of practice" theory and Peterson/Bennett's local/translocal/virtual scene model to argue that cassette tapes persist as hybrid material artefacts across DIY music scenes in Japan, Australia, and the USA — and that their significance cannot be understood from any single scene or context alone.

**Sources**: `Duester Bennett How does materiality bite back.pdf`

**Last updated**: 2026-04-25

## Theoretical framework

### Magaudda's circuit of practice
Magaudda (2011) argues that the vinyl record's return reflects a "crisis of authenticity" in digital music — listeners sought the material, culturally saturated experience of analog formats. The authors extend this: Magaudda's model only addresses music consumption, not the full circuit of DIY production, distribution, and non-monetary exchange (gifting, trading, scene participation). (source: `Duester Bennett How does materiality bite back.pdf`)

### Peterson and Bennett's three-tier scene model
Scenes operate at three levels simultaneously:
- **Local** — face-to-face community, record stores, venues, concerts
- **Translocal** — connections across cities and countries (DIY tours, mail-order, distribution)
- **Virtual** — Bandcamp, social media, podcasts like Tabs Out

Cassette tapes function as connective tissue across all three levels. (source: `Duester Bennett How does materiality bite back.pdf`)

### Cassette hybridity
The cassette is a hybrid format in multiple senses: re-recordable, small and sturdy for creative packaging, cheap to ship, usable for both recording and distribution. Its hybridity is not new (it has existed since the 1960s) — digital download codes added to current cassette releases are just one more layer of this longstanding hybridity, not a fundamental transformation. (source: `Duester Bennett How does materiality bite back.pdf`)

## Case study 1: Oxtail Recordings (local → virtual)

Mike Nigro founded Oxtail Recordings around 2013, influenced by the Tabs Out cassette podcast (Delaware). His label trajectory: Delaware local scene → translocal US/Canada touring → virtual Bandcamp distribution → Sydney local scene after relocation. Cassettes are the format because "for this type of music — experimental, synthesizer, noise, home-made bedroom music — cassette tape has that history and that lineage. When you release that kind of music on that format you are participating in that history." Limited runs of 50 per release; focus on audio and visual quality; donates proceeds to First Nations causes. (source: `Duester Bennett How does materiality bite back.pdf`)

The circuit flows *outward*: local skills developed in Delaware extended to translocal and virtual contexts through touring and Bandcamp.

## Case study 2: Muzan Editions (virtual → local)

Joshua Stefane (Canadian) founded Muzan Editions in Osaka in 2016 after discovering a virtual Bandcamp ambient/tape scene (Constellation Tatsu, A Giant Fern). The label is run by expats (Canadian and German) who chose cassettes because that's what the ambient scene uses — vinyl was too expensive, CDs unappealing. Their challenge: Japanese peers don't share the Western nostalgia for tapes ("cassettes in Japan? Forget about it!"). 98% of Muzan's customers are from abroad.

The circuit flows *inward*: starting from virtual ambient scenes, the label attempts to build local Kansai connections — using cassettes as tools to attract international artists and create community in Japan. (source: `Duester Bennett How does materiality bite back.pdf`)

---

## Key argument against "cassette scene"

The authors argue that lumping all cassette users into a "cassette scene" is reductive — it creates artificial relationships based on the smallest common denominator (format used). Instead, cassettes should be understood as circulating within pre-existing scenes (ambient, punk, noise, electronic) that each have their own values, geographies, and social dynamics. (source: `Duester Bennett How does materiality bite back.pdf`)
