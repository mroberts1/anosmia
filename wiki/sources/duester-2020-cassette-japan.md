# Obsolete Technology? The Significance of the Cassette Format in Twenty-First-Century Japan — Duester (2020)

**Summary**: An ethnographic study of the cassette tape's status in contemporary Japan, arguing that the mainstream "cassette revival" narrative misses the format's complex, multifaceted reality — split between the enka industry, DIY music scenes, and a commercialized Cassette Store Day that alienated actual scene participants.

**Sources**: `978-3-030-44659-8_10.pdf`

**Last updated**: 2026-04-25

## Bibliographic details

- Author: Benjamin Düster, Griffith University, Brisbane
- Chapter 10 in: Tofalvy & Barna (eds.), *Popular Music, Technology, and the Changing Media Ecosystem*, Springer, 2020
- DOI: 10.1007/978-3-030-44659-8_10
- Based on four months of fieldwork in Japan (Oct 2018–Jan 2019): 32 interviews, participant observation in Tokyo, Nagoya, Osaka, Kyoto, Nara, Kobe, Fukuoka

## Core argument

The cassette's presence in 21st-century Japan cannot be reduced to a simple revival narrative. It occupies multiple, often contradictory niches simultaneously: a legacy format for the conservative enka industry, a grassroots DIY medium for punk and noise scenes, and a kawaii merchandise item marketed to young women through Cassette Store Day Japan. These three contexts have nearly nothing in common except the physical object. (source: `978-3-030-44659-8_10.pdf`)

---

## The enka industry

Japan's domestic cassette production has been declining since 1988 (when CD output first surpassed cassette output). By 2018, 441,000 cassettes were produced nationally — almost entirely for the *enka* genre: conservative Japanese popular music with an aging fanbase that grew up with the format. Enka releases included 155 new singles and only 9 albums on cassette in 2018.

Japanese cassette production plants served both enka labels and DIY punk scenes. The company **Yabuki Rokuon Kōbō** (Koganei, near Tokyo) — run by two men in an apartment basement for 26 years — exemplifies this: its walls were covered in DIY punk stickers while it produced enka singles. It closed in June 2018 after one owner died. (source: `978-3-030-44659-8_10.pdf`)

---

## DIY scenes and imported cassettes

As Japanese domestic cassette manufacturers close or move away from non-enka production, DIY labels have shifted to importing from North American suppliers (NAC, Duplication.ca) or using Japanese middlemen like Cassette Express (run by Vinyl Star International). The reasons: color options (Japan's plants offer only white shells for enka; North American plants offer 25–50+ colors), and convenient services including J-card printing and assembly.

The noise and experimental music scene has engaged with cassettes since the 1980s. Akifumi Nakajima's G.R.O.S.S. label was influential for its experimental cassette releases; David Novak's *Japanoise* (2013) analyzes cassettes as "calling cards" in North American noise scenes. Contemporary labels like Lust Vessel (Kantō) continue the aesthetic. (source: `978-3-030-44659-8_10.pdf`)

---

## Cassette Store Day Japan

CSD Japan (from 2016) was organized by Vinyl Star International / Cassette Express as a marketing instrument for their business. Its 2017 ambassador was Hatsune Miku (the Vocaloid virtual idol); its 2018 ambassador was the all-female band Scandal — Duester notes CSD Japan explicitly targeted a female consumer demographic, with cassettes marketed as *kawaii* (cute) collectible toys, exploiting the established aesthetic of female cuteness in Japanese consumer culture.

Members of actual DIY scenes rejected the event as corporate and disconnected. Toda Takeshi (Buy Nowers Club blog): "Normal people that usually have nothing to do with cassettes all of a sudden take part. It's nothing for people that buy cassettes on a day-to-day basis." (source: `978-3-030-44659-8_10.pdf`)

---

## Key conclusion

The cassette's status in Japan is hybrid and context-dependent. The mainstream revival narrative captures only CSD Japan and major-label releases — missing the continuous DIY use in independent scenes that never stopped. Labels like Duenn (Fukuoka) and VLZ Produkt (Tokyo) refuse to attach download codes to their cassette releases, treating the format as a serious audio medium in its own right. (source: `978-3-030-44659-8_10.pdf`)
