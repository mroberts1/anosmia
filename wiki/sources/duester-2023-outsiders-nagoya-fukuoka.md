# Outsiders in Outsider Cities? Expatriates in the DIY Music Scenes of Nagoya and Fukuoka — Duester (2023)

**Summary**: Examines how Western expatriates disproportionately document, connect, and promote DIY music in Nagoya and Fukuoka — Japanese cities overshadowed by the Tokyo/Osaka axis — and how their outsider position creates both productive bridges and cultural frictions.

## The Tokyo/Osaka problem in Japan music scholarship

Academic and journalistic coverage of Japanese independent music overwhelmingly focuses on Tokyo (Kantō) and Osaka (Kansai). Key works by Novak (*Japanoise*, 2013), Overell (grindcore in Osaka), and Matsue (Tokyo hardcore) reinforce this bias. Smaller cities host established scenes that "participate in the overall production and distribution of independent music in Japan and on an international scale" but remain largely undocumented. (source: `978-3-031-08615-1_13.pdf`)

---

## Nagoya

Japan's fourth-largest city, historically an economic hub between Kanto and Kansai — rarely framed as a cultural destination. Key traits of its music scene:

- **Cross-genre community**: fewer venues means bands and audiences from different genres mix more than in Tokyo, where scenes fragment into genre-specific niches
- **Record store as hub**: Takehiko Yamada's File Under Records (est. 2002) + label Knew Noise Recordings (roster includes Bo Ningen, Comanechi — Japanese bands based in London)
- **Galaxy Train**: one of Japan's longest-running DIY labels (cassettes and 7-inches since 1998), with a punk-rooted DIY ethos; operators hand-deliver releases to local stores

Ian F. Martin (UK journalist, Tokyo-based): Tokyo is "too big" — "nothing is holding the scene together, it's falling apart." Bar Ripple's operator Nobu contrasts Tokyo bands' "complex, clinically delivered rhythms" with Nagoya bands' preference for "raw energy." (source: `978-3-031-08615-1_13.pdf`)

## Fukuoka

Located on Kyushu island, geographically separated from the Kanto–Kansai touring axis — artists must make a deliberate detour to play Fukuoka. Historical music identity:

- **Mentai Rock** (late 1970s–mid 1990s): Sheena & the Rokkets, The Roosters, SONHOUSE — blues rock to new wave, named after mentaiko (local delicacy)
- **Hakata No Wave** (mid-1990s onward): newer experimental indie rock, spearheaded by Number Girl (signed to Toshiba EMI)
- **Muzan Editions**: expat-run cassette label (Canadian Joshua Stefane + German Andreas Holderbach) operating in Kansai but distributing globally; 98% of customers are overseas

Holderbach on approaching Japanese artists: "It's always positive... 'What?! Me?!'" — Japanese artists' self-critical modesty and deference require trust-building before collaboration. Speaking Japanese is essential. (source: `978-3-031-08615-1_13.pdf`)

## Expatriates as connectors

Duester argues expatriates fill a specific structural gap: they bring "Western DIY ethos" — proactive, outward-facing, used to international networking — and combine it with the "introspective and at times low-key artistry" of Japanese peers, creating unusual synergies.

Key expatriate contributions:
- **Ian F. Martin** (British, Tokyo): blog *Burn Your Hometown* — bicycle journey through every Japanese prefecture documenting local scenes; also ran Call and Response Records
- **Matthew Bedford** (American): *Muso Japan* blog (2012+), ~200 reviews of indie/shoegaze from Japan; hosted *Muso Asia* on DKFM Shoegaze Radio
- **Muzan Editions**: attract international artists to Kansai, create releases that travel globally

The "gaijin" (外人, "outside person") position carries both disadvantage (cultural distance, language barriers) and privilege (freedom from local social expectations and hierarchy). (source: `978-3-031-08615-1_13.pdf`)
