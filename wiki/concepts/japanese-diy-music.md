# Japanese DIY Music

**Summary**: The landscape of independent and DIY music in Japan — spanning noise, punk, electronic, ambient, and experimental genres — and the particular role of cassette tapes, regional scenes, expatriate participants, and the tension between Japan's mainstream music market and its underground.

**Sources**: `978-3-030-44659-8_10.pdf`, `978-3-031-08615-1_13.pdf`, `Duester Bennett How does materiality bite back.pdf`, `Duester Cassette Comeback.pdf`

**Last updated**: 2026-04-25

---

## Japan's music market context

Japan is the world's second-largest music market. Its revenue structure is unusual by global standards: 79% physical media (2018), with CDs dominant. Streaming only surpassed 50% of digital revenue for the first time in 2018. Vinyl has grown from ~400,000 units (2014) to 2.6 million (2023). (source: `978-3-030-44659-8_10.pdf`, `Duester Cassette Comeback.pdf`)

This physical-media dominance shapes DIY scenes: releasing on CD is often more culturally legible to Japanese audiences than cassette or vinyl. The Osaka-based label Muzan Editions (expat-run) found that 98% of its cassette customers were from abroad — Japanese peers often asked "Why are you doing tapes? Don't you have CDs?" (source: `Duester Bennett How does materiality bite back.pdf`)

## Cassette tapes in Japan: the enka/punk split

Domestic cassette production in Japan has been in continuous decline since 1988. By 2018, output was 441,000 units, almost entirely for the *enka* genre. By 2023, only 119,000 units produced, and enka cassette singles collapsed from 155 new titles (2018) to just 3 (2023). Japanese domestic plants offered only white shells; DIY scenes requiring colored cassettes now import from NAC (Missouri) and Duplication.ca (Canada).

**Enka**: Conservative post-war Japanese popular music; aging fanbase; nationalistic lyrical content; female singers typically in kimono. Cassette singles (not albums) were the dominant format. A genre-format ecosystem now largely transitioning to CD.

**Punk/noise/DIY**: The same production plants served both enka and DIY music. Yabuki Rokuon Kōbō (Koganei, Tokyo) — its walls covered in punk band stickers — made enka singles and DIY punk tapes for 26 years before closing in 2018. This cross-genre proximity was enabled by the cassette's material hybridity. (source: `978-3-030-44659-8_10.pdf`)

## The noise scene

Japan's noise scene has global reach and a cassette heritage stretching back to the 1980s. Key figures:
- **Merzbow** (Masami Akita): prolific split releases over 40 years
- **Hijokaidan**: early Osaka noise, instrumental in Kansai's international profile
- **G.R.O.S.S.** (Akifumi Nakajima): influential 1990s experimental cassette label; releases like the Three Temples triple-cassette set (hexagonal black corrugated plastic with linen rope)
- **Fushitsusha, Mainliner, Hadaka no Rarīzu (Les Rallizes Dénudés), Boris**: key projects from Kanto/Kansai

David Novak (*Japanoise*, 2013) traces how Osaka transformed from "outsider city" to internationally acclaimed noise center through transnational cassette circulation. (source: `978-3-031-08615-1_13.pdf`)

## Regional scenes: Nagoya and Fukuoka

Academic and journalistic attention overwhelmingly focuses on Tokyo and Osaka. Two overlooked cities with established scenes:

### Nagoya
Japan's fourth-largest city; economic hub between Kanto and Kansai; rarely framed as a cultural destination. Scene characteristics:
- Cross-genre mixing (fewer venues forces broader coexistence)
- File Under Records (est. 2002) + Knew Noise Recordings label as hubs
- Galaxy Train label (cassettes and 7-inches since 1998) — one of Japan's longest-running DIY labels
- Dominant genres: indie rock, shoegaze, punk, singer-songwriter

Ian F. Martin (UK journalist, Tokyo): Tokyo bands have "complex, clinically delivered rhythms" born of competitive pressure; Nagoya bands prioritize "raw energy" and cross-genre community. (source: `978-3-031-08615-1_13.pdf`)

### Fukuoka
On Kyushu island; geographically isolated from the Kanto–Kansai touring axis — artists must make a deliberate trip. Historical identity:
- **Mentai Rock** (late 1970s–mid 1990s): Sheena & the Rokkets, The Roosters, SONHOUSE; blues rock to new wave; named after mentaiko (local pollock roe delicacy)
- **Hakata No Wave** (mid-1990s): experimental indie rock; Number Girl (signed to Toshiba EMI)
- Contemporary labels: Duenn (refuses download codes; cassette-only), active in noise/electronic

(source: `978-3-031-08615-1_13.pdf`)

## Expatriates in Japanese DIY music

Expatriates play a disproportionate structural role in Japanese DIY scenes — particularly in smaller cities and in cassette culture. Their "gaijin" (outsider) position carries both disadvantage (cultural distance, language barriers) and privilege (freedom from local social hierarchy). Key examples:

- **Ian F. Martin** (British, Tokyo): *Burn Your Hometown* blog — cycled through every Japanese prefecture documenting local scenes; ran Call and Response Records
- **Matthew Bedford** (American): *Muso Japan* blog (200+ reviews, indie/shoegaze focus); *Muso Asia* radio series on DKFM Shoegaze Radio
- **Muzan Editions** (Joshua Stefane, Canadian; Andreas Holderbach, German; based in Kansai): ambient/noise cassette label; uses cassettes to attract international artists and build local community; 98% of customers overseas

Expatriates function as translators — connecting local Japanese artists to international audiences, and bringing international artists and norms into local scenes. Speaking Japanese is cited as essential to building genuine trust with Japanese artists, who often display cultural modesty ("What?! Me?!") when approached for collaborations. (source: `978-3-031-08615-1_13.pdf`)

## Cassette Store Day Japan

CSD Japan (from 2016), organized by Vinyl Star International / Cassette Express, attempted to introduce cassettes to young Japanese consumers as *kawaii* collectible items. Ambassadors were Hatsune Miku (2017) and the band Scandal (2018) — both targeting a young female consumer demographic. Scene members consistently rejected CSD as corporate and irrelevant to their practice. (source: `978-3-030-44659-8_10.pdf`)
