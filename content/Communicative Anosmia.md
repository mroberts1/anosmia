## What it is

Communicative anosmia names the structural condition of perfume advertising: the viewer cannot smell the product. The term, coined by [[Halszka Leleń]], borrows from the clinical condition of anosmia — the inability to perceive smell — and applies it to the communicative situation of any audio-visual medium attempting to market a scent. No technical workaround exists. The ad cannot transmit the defining property of what it is selling.

## Why it matters

This is not a limitation to be overcome by better production values. It is constitutive of the genre. Every formal choice in a perfume commercial — the narrative structure, the casting, the montage strategy, the literary allusions, the body-in-motion — is a response to communicative anosmia. Understanding the constraint explains the aesthetic: the genre's peculiar richness, its density of cultural reference, its investment in immersion and intersubjectivity, all follow from the impossibility of the core sales task. Communicative anosmia also has an ideological dimension: because the ad cannot demonstrate the product, it must substitute desire — which is where gender myth, racial coding, and the "false obvious" enter.

## Key points

- The viewer of a perfume commercial is, by definition, sensory-deprived with respect to the product's defining quality [[Communicative Anosmia and Poetics of Storytelling in Perfume Commercials]].
- Directors do not try to describe scent; they compensate through "heightening the target viewer's intersubjective immersion in the filmic storyworlds" [[Communicative Anosmia and Poetics of Storytelling in Perfume Commercials]].
- The gap between ad and product is structural and temporal: campaign launches often precede the product's market availability, and even after launch, a time-lapse separates viewing from access to the scent [[Communicative Anosmia and Poetics of Storytelling in Perfume Commercials]].
- Visual substitution strategies include: palimpsestic narrative layering, celebrity intertextuality, the body-in-motion as diffusion equivalent, synesthetic sign-complexes, and Jakobson's poetic function (selection and combination creating equivalences) [[Communicative Anosmia and Poetics of Storytelling in Perfume Commercials]].
- Kress's multimodal semiotics explains how this substitution works: meaning arises from "fusion of form and meaning" across image, sound, and text simultaneously — no single channel carries the message [[Communicative Anosmia and Poetics of Storytelling in Perfume Commercials]].
- The absent scent also underwrites ideological substitution: "perfume does not possess the direct functionality of elevating personal status or creating a better life," so advertising provides "imaginative solutions to real social problems" through visual narrative [[The False Obvious Gender Myth in Perfume Commercials]].
- Audiences are offered "vicarious indulgence in the good life through the image alone" — the fiction compensates for what the commodity cannot deliver [[The False Obvious Gender Myth in Perfume Commercials]].
- Coleridge's "willing suspension of disbelief" is engineered in reverse: narrative absorption prevents the viewer from maintaining a skeptical, advertising-resistant distance [[Communicative Anosmia and Poetics of Storytelling in Perfume Commercials]].
- Baudelaire's *Correspondences* (1857) is the historical origin of the equation between scent and synesthetic transcendence that perfume advertising inherits: "There are perfumes as cool as the flesh of children / Sweet as oboes, green as meadows" — an olfactory experience rendered through every sense but smell [[Communicative Anosmia and Poetics of Storytelling in Perfume Commercials]].

## Evidence across sources

**Leleń (2022)** theorizes communicative anosmia directly and builds her entire analytical framework on it. The compensatory strategies she identifies — palimpsestic narrative, narrative immersion through perspectivization, multimodal sign-complexes, celebrity intertextuality — are responses to the structural impossibility of transmitting scent. Her case studies (Rihanna Reb'l Fleur, Chanel No. 5, Kenzo World, Miss Dior) each demonstrate different solutions: palindromic structure, fairy-tale layering, deliberate anti-eroticism, poetic quotation.

**Deng and Shi (2024)** do not name communicative anosmia, but their analysis of how Dior commercials function as ideological compensation premises the same structural gap. Their key move — the symptomatic reading of what is absent (actual scent function, real social contradiction) — only makes sense if the ad cannot sell scent on scent's terms. The "imaginative solution to real problems" that perfume advertising offers is the ideological face of what Leleń describes as narrative immersion. The two frameworks converge here: both see the ad's narrative richness as a compensation for a fundamental impossibility. They diverge on what kind of work that compensation is doing — craft versus ideology.

## Open questions

- Does communicative anosmia apply equally to print and digital perfume advertising, or is it specific to audio-visual short-film formats?
- What changes when consumers can smell the product first (in-store, at a counter, via sample) and then encounter the ad? Does anosmia remain structurally constitutive, or does it become partial?
- Is there a counter-tradition — perfume campaigns that attempt description rather than immersion? What happens to them?
- The historical narrative: Leleń cites Baudelaire as origin. What was perfume advertising doing before the symbolist synesthesia framework became available?

## Prompts

- Leleń argues that Kenzo World (Spike Jonze, 2016) "defies" the erotically underpinned pattern of perfume advertising through deliberately non-sensual choreography. Is that a genuine escape from communicative anosmia's logic, or does it just find a different immersive strategy? What does "subversion" mean inside a genre structurally committed to compensatory desire?
- If communicative anosmia is constitutive of the genre, what would it mean to make a "honest" perfume ad — one that acknowledged it couldn't convey the scent? Does any such example exist? What would it look like?
- Deng and Shi argue the gap between ad and product is ideologically productive: the commodity can't deliver the independence it promises, so the narrative does. Does this mean the ideological work *requires* communicative anosmia, or merely exploits it? If perfume could be transmitted — smelled through the screen — would the gender myth dissolve or just find another vehicle?
