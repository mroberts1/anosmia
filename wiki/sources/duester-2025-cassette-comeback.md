# The 'Cassette Comeback': Debunking a Tenacious Narrative — Duester (2025)

**Summary**: A systematic critique of the "cassette revival" media narrative, arguing that cassettes never completely disappeared, that commercial sales statistics miss the DIY scene entirely, and that the perseverance of cassettes is explained by material and cultural factors far more complex than nostalgia.

**Sources**: `Duester Cassette Comeback.pdf`

**Last updated**: 2026-04-25

---

## Bibliographic details

- Author: Benjamin Duester, Department of Musicology, Georg August University of Göttingen
- Journal: *Continuum: Journal of Media & Cultural Studies*, 2025
- DOI: 10.1080/10304312.2025.2451943
- Based on 42 expert interviews across Tokyo, Melbourne (Naarm), and Los Angeles, 2018–2019

## Core argument

The "cassette comeback" narrative has been recycled by news outlets since the late 2000s without substantive change. It relies on commercial sales figures that: (a) conflate novelty merchandise (Guardians of the Galaxy soundtrack, Metallica demo reissues) with genuine DIY use; (b) miss the massive volume of DIY releases sold through Bandcamp and Discogs. Cassettes were never dead — they were in continuous use in DIY music scenes throughout the 2000s while mainstream media declared them obsolete. (source: `Duester Cassette Comeback.pdf`)

---

## The statistics problem

- US cassette sales rose from 129,000 (2016) → 174,000 (2017) → 219,000 (2018) per Nielsen — cited as evidence of revival
- But Discogs data shows ~2,500 new cassette titles in punk alone in 2017; at a conservative average of 50 copies each, that's 125,000 cassettes in one genre from one platform — not captured by Nielsen
- Bandcamp retailed ~500,000 cassettes in 2021; Discogs sold ~286,000 in the same year
- US and Australian industry associations (RIAA, ARIA) categorize cassettes under "other" with SVCDs and DVDs — figures decline but details are obscured

The cassette's "death" in 2007 was proclaimed in the same year that other articles noted its continued use by DIY devotees. Contradictory declarations were simultaneous. (source: `Duester Cassette Comeback.pdf`)

## Who kept cassettes alive through the 2000s

Three non-obvious sustaining uses:
1. **Religious ministries** — Joyce Meyer Ministries alone ordered 250,000 blank tapes per week for cassette sermons; similar patterns in Egypt, Yemen, and Iran
2. **Cars** — cassette decks persisted in vehicles until the late 2000s (final factory-fitted US model: 2010 Lexus SC 430); the second-hand car market extended this further
3. **Language education, prisons, police recording**

These kept manufacturers like National Audio Company (NAC, Springfield, Missouri) operational during the trough. DIY music demand then grew to fill the gap as religious cassette use declined. (source: `Duester Cassette Comeback.pdf`)

## The production infrastructure

**NAC** (National Audio Company, Missouri) and **Duplication.ca** (Canada) are now the principal global sources for cassette duplication services. NAC produced the high-profile "revival" releases (Guardians of the Galaxy, Metallica) as well as DIY runs. Its magnetic tape supplier terminated production in 2014; NAC improvised by repurposing credit card magnetic strip machinery before introducing its Super Ferric tape in 2019. **Mulann** (France) introduced the Fox audio tape in 2019 for the European market.

Tom Martin (Comfort 35, Melbourne) initially sourced cassettes from Lifeshaper, a local Christian duplication facility — creating an unlikely connection between evangelical ministry and Melbourne's noise scene. (source: `Duester Cassette Comeback.pdf`)

---

## The hardware problem

Working cassette players are increasingly scarce and expensive. Used high-quality decks (Sony, etc.) that were plentiful in the 2000s are now rare. Only two new tape decks are currently manufactured: the TASCAM 202MKVII ($499) and a TEAC equivalent ($390) — both with inferior plastic construction compared to older units. Cheap USB "converter" players (repurposed digitization devices, as low as $7 from Chinese manufacturers) are widely available but sonically inadequate. The NINM Lab IT'S OK Bluetooth cassette player ($89) was found to be a repackaged Skywin device with mono audio. (source: `Duester Cassette Comeback.pdf`)

---

## Japan update (2023 data)

Japan produced only 119,000 cassettes domestically in 2023, all for enka. Enka cassette singles collapsed from 155 new titles (2018) to just 3 (2023). CDs have replaced cassettes even within enka. Japanese DIY scenes now source entirely from overseas. (source: `Duester Cassette Comeback.pdf`)

## Key voices

- **Gabe Holcombe** (Lillerne Tape Club, Chicago, founded 2007 — the cassette's "fatal year"): "We've been waiting for the 'cassette boom' to bust for years, but it seems fairly stable in its own, tiny way"
- **Lisa Foster** (Guestroom Records): "The Cassette Comeback Isn't What You Think It Is" — growth driven by vinyl price increases, not pure nostalgia
- **Big Love Records** (Tokyo): handpainted shelf sign reading "TAPES Never Went Away"

(source: `Duester Cassette Comeback.pdf`)
