**Summary**
Deng and Shi argue that perfume commercials do not merely reflect gender norms but actively construct and naturalize them as myth. Through content analysis of 45 major-brand commercials (CK, Dior, Chanel, Versace), they find the gender ideals on offer are specifically those of heteronormative bourgeois white Western culture: 95% white protagonists, near-universal heterosexual interaction, erotic coding of both male and female bodies. Drawing on Barthes's myth analysis, Hall's representation theory, and Butler's gender performativity, they show how these myths achieve the status of "false obvious" — appearing natural rather than constructed. Even ads framed as celebrating female independence remain structured around the male gaze.

**Key points**
- Perfume's unvisualizable scent forces ads to substitute erotic imagery — smell becomes sex
- 64% of the 45-ad sample showed physical nudity; 73% showed explicit or implied sexual behavior
- 95% used white protagonists; non-white actors appeared in only 2 cases (CK One and Versace Man Eau Fraiche)
- Settings favor modern metropolitan luxury (castles, pools, fireworks) to signal Western bourgeois aspiration
- Barthes: gender myths in perfume ads achieve "false obvious" status — constructed meaning presented as natural fact
- Miss Dior (Portman): black-and-white → color transition codes perfume as liberation, but "independent" femininity still targets male desire
- J'adore (Theron): dual mythological layers — naked erotic body (first level) → goddess of independence (second level)
- Butler: gender identity is "repeated stylization of the body" within a rigid regulatory frame; ads provide the template
- Perfume ads achieve a communicative effect similar to pornography: audiences are incited to perform gender norms to become "sexy"

**Claude's notes**
The symptomatic reading method — examining what is *absent* from ads as much as what is present — is the sharpest move here. The case that Miss Dior's "independent femininity" is still fundamentally structured to please men, traced through Butler and de Beauvoir, is well-argued. One caution: the 45-ad sample is self-described as based on "search engine results" and explicitly non-exhaustive. The quantitative findings (95% white, etc.) are striking but rest on a small, non-random sample and should be treated as indicative rather than definitive. Published in *Cultural and Religious Studies* by David Publishing, a house with known quality-control issues; the theoretical framework is solid but quantitative claims warrant extra scrutiny.
