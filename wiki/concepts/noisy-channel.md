# Noisy Channel

**Summary**: Adam Harper's aesthetic framework for understanding appreciation of recording imperfections — borrowed from communication theory, it treats "the music" as signal and recording imperfections as noise in a channel, making the relationship between them the aesthetic object.

**Sources**: `What was Lo-Fi .pdf`

**Last updated**: 2026-04-25

---

## The framework

Harper draws on communication theory's signal/noise distinction to theorize the aesthetics of [[lo-fi]]. In a noisy channel of communication — e.g., a radio conversation accompanied by interference — the channel transmits a signal (music) through noise (recording imperfections). The imperfections are differentiable from "the music" but provide their own information about the transmission. (source: `What was Lo-Fi .pdf`)

Recording imperfections include:
- Tape hiss, vinyl crackle, electrical hum
- Frequency responses perceived as "poor"
- Performance errors ("wrong" notes)
- Environmental sounds captured in the recording

These are not simply negative; Marie Thompson's communication-theory argument applies: "noise may play an alternative role — it may be a source of new information that is of potential interest... noise has the potential to unlock new information... noise's 'positive' role... coexists with its 'negative,' destructive role." (source: `What was Lo-Fi .pdf`)

## Key perceptual claims

**1. Imperfections are perceived as imperfect.** Even when enjoyed, they are heard as nominally or allegedly imperfect — "formerly unwanted sound" or "sound unwanted by others." As Thompson puts it, noise in noise music "can only ever be a simulation of noise 'proper.'" (source: `What was Lo-Fi .pdf`)

**2. Imperfections are differentiable from "the music."** They form a layer or surface — an outer framing for the signal. Sometimes listeners "listen past" the noise to reach the music; the lo-fi inversion is Alexandra Supper's "listening for the hiss." But typically it is the *relationship* between signal and noise that matters aesthetically. (source: `What was Lo-Fi .pdf`)

**3. Imperfections can become instruments.** At the extreme, the noise fully integrates: Daniel Johnston's tape machine becomes "an instrument in itself, an ever-present rumble." Pavement's "sonic shuffling and dicking about is part of the song." Robert Pollard of Guided By Voices: "if I thought a song was too creamy or poppy, then I would fuck it up. We'd put a drone through it or drench it in feedback." (source: `What was Lo-Fi .pdf`)

## Imperfection and authenticity

Recording imperfections are treated in lo-fi discourse as indicators of authenticity — evidence of a lack of mediation by industry standards. But this creates a paradox: audible noise from recording technology *is* evidence of mediation. Harper, after Ryan Hibbett: "the most direct evidence of production connotes its absence." Emily Dolan's resolution: the "honesty" comes not from the illusion of unmediated communication but from "openly emphasizing the process of mediation." (source: `What was Lo-Fi .pdf`)

## Dramas of the imperfect signal

Harper's term for the most intense form of noisy-channel appreciation. Signal (music) and noise (imperfection) become antagonists — the signal communicating, the noise threatening to undermine it. This supplies drama and narrative: "pitting the presence and creativity of signal against forces that render it imperfect and would see it fall short." (source: `What was Lo-Fi .pdf`)

Examples:
- **Daniel Johnston**: "the force of his desire to push beyond the structural impossibility of this body producing a singing voice and this chord organ producing music" — Shank's three-listen progression from anger to appreciation
- **Bob Dylan bootlegs** (*Great White Wonder*): "these tracks sound furtive and bootlegged... poor sound, however, cannot mask what are some fine — even great — songs"
- **Folk revival recordings**: "the most poignant sound on the record is the background roar of a tractor... if the context of the music is decay, the music itself speaks of a vivid life"
- **Liz Phair** (*Girly Sound* tapes): deliberately impaired recording to keep lyrics from her parents — "an unbearable nervous calm that's unsettling and beautiful"

(source: `What was Lo-Fi .pdf`)

## Disability and impairment

Harper connects this to disability studies. Imperfect sound parallels "culturally stigmatized bodily difference" — deviation from what is understood as normal. Sterne: "impairment may be a blockage, a failure, a defect, but it is also a supplement, rich with texture and potential meaning." The "damaged voice" in popular music (Laurie Stras) — preferred precisely because damage connotes authority and authenticity — is a precursor to the aesthetics of recording imperfection. (source: `What was Lo-Fi .pdf`)

## Why this does not apply to lofi hip hop

[[lofi-hip-hop]] lacks the "drama of the imperfect signal." Its mild analog texture is relaxing rather than confrontational, and the listener is peacefully engaged in other tasks (studying, relaxing). There is no antagonism between signal and noise, no politics of authenticity, no experience of struggle. (source: `What was Lo-Fi .pdf`)
