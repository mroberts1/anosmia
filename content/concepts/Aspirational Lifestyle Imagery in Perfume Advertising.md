## What it is

Perfume commercials substitute aspirational lifestyle imagery for any direct description of the product. Ads sell an imagined world — luxurious, sensual, liberated, or natural — with the perfume functioning as the key that unlocks it. The substitution is openly acknowledged in audience reception theory: viewers know the product will not deliver the promised life, and buy the image anyway.

Two main categories dominate (Tsutsumi): **luxury** (castles, balls, swimming pools, European cityscapes, cars, glamorous parties) and **nature** (floral, elemental, oceanic, "fresh" countryside). Most ads combine the two, with nature settings elevated by a luxury framing.

## Why it matters

Aspirational imagery is the primary mechanism by which communicative anosmia is addressed. Because the smell cannot be shown, ads build a world the viewer wants to inhabit — and position the perfume as the portal. This is also where gender and racial coding concentrates most visibly: the aspirational world has a specific demographic shape.

## Key points

- Perfume moved from elite to mass market in the 20th century but its advertising retained elite aspiration — the scent as "passport to the world of delights" (Reinarz, cited in Leleń)
- Settings in the Deng & Shi sample (45 ads) are "almost all" modern metropolises, featuring "castles, balls, fireworks, and swimming pools to demonstrate the dignity and superiority of the sophisticated and dreamy Western middle class"
- Winship (quoted in Deng & Shi): "Without bothering to buy the product we can vicariously indulge in the good life through the image alone. This is the compensation for the experience you do not and cannot have"
- The aspiration is racially coded: the luxurious world in mainstream Western ads is constructed around white protagonists and European or generic-global settings
- "Natural" aspirational imagery — florals, ocean, countryside — maps onto feminized aesthetic values and the nature-themed ad subcategory
- "Luxury" imagery serves both male and female ads but with distinct gender coding: male ads emphasize conquest and dominance; female ads emphasize elegance and freedom
- NCT DOJAEJUNG "Perfume" MV: filmed in Thailand, not Europe; nature-leaning (floral rooms, gardens, falling petals) but includes European-style mansion and swimming pool; aspirational register maintained, geographic frame shifted to acknowledge Asian context^[#source/secondary]
- The Thailand choice partially decouples the luxury aspiration from its Western European coding while retaining the structural logic of aspiration

## Evidence across sources

All three sources describe aspirational imagery as a defining feature of perfume ads. Leleń focuses on how aspiration is delivered through narrative immersion. Deng & Shi focus on how aspirational settings encode race and class ideology. Tsutsumi examines how a K-Pop group navigates the convention — adopting it while partially relocating it geographically.

No source contests the centrality of aspirational imagery. The differences are in what follows from it.
